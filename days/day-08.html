Trigonometric Substitution

Trigonometric substitution is used to evaluate integrals involving square roots of quadratic expressions. The key is to use the Pythagorean identities to eliminate the radical.

Substitution Guide

  • For $\sqrt{a^2 - x^2}$: use $x = a\sin(\theta)$, $dx = a\cos(\theta)\,d\theta$
  • For $\sqrt{a^2 + x^2}$: use $x = a\tan(\theta)$, $dx = a\sec^2(\theta)\,d\theta$
  • For $\sqrt{x^2 - a^2}$: use $x = a\sec(\theta)$, $dx = a\sec(\theta)\tan(\theta)\,d\theta$

After substitution, draw a right triangle to express trigonometric functions back in terms of $x$.

Example 1.1

Problem: Evaluate $\int \frac{dx}{\sqrt{9+4x^2}}$

Figure — Trig substitution reference triangle

The right triangle labels come from the substitution $2x = 3\tan\theta$, giving hypotenuse $\sqrt{9+4x^2}$.

Click to see the solution

Step 1: Identify the substitution

We have $\sqrt{9+4x^2} = \sqrt{a^2+x^2}$ form with $a = 3$.

Use the substitution $2x = 3\tan(\theta)$, so $x = \frac{3}{2}\tan(\theta)$:

$$dx = \frac{3}{2}\sec^2(\theta)\,d\theta$$

Step 2: Simplify the radical

$$\sqrt{9+4x^2} = \sqrt{9 + 4 \cdot \frac{9}{4}\tan^2(\theta)}$$ $$= \sqrt{9(1+\tan^2(\theta))}$$ $$= 3\sqrt{\sec^2(\theta)}$$ $$= 3\sec(\theta)$$

Step 3: Substitute

$$\int \frac{dx}{\sqrt{9+4x^2}} = \int \frac{\frac{3}{2}\sec^2(\theta)\,d\theta}{3\sec(\theta)}$$ $$= \frac{1}{2}\int \sec(\theta)\,d\theta$$ $$= \frac{1}{2}\ln|\sec(\theta) + \tan(\theta)| + C$$

Step 4: Back-substitute

From $x = \frac{3}{2}\tan(\theta)$, we have $\tan(\theta) = \frac{2x}{3}$.

Using the right triangle: opposite $= 2x$, adjacent $= 3$, hypotenuse $= \sqrt{9+4x^2}$:

$$\sec(\theta) = \frac{\sqrt{9+4x^2}}{3}$$

Therefore:

$$\sec(\theta) + \tan(\theta) = \frac{\sqrt{9+4x^2}}{3} + \frac{2x}{3} = \frac{\sqrt{9+4x^2} + 2x}{3}$$

Step 5: Final result

$$= \frac{1}{2}\ln\left|\frac{\sqrt{9+4x^2} + 2x}{3}\right| + C$$ $$= \frac{1}{2}\ln|\sqrt{9+4x^2} + 2x| - \frac{1}{2}\ln(3) + C$$

Combining constants:

$$= \frac{1}{2}\ln|\sqrt{9+4x^2} + 2x| + C'$$

Final Answer:

$$\boxed{\int \frac{dx}{\sqrt{9+4x^2}} = \frac{1}{2}\ln|\sqrt{9+4x^2} + 2x| + C}$$

Example 1.2

Problem: Evaluate $\int \frac{dx}{x^2\sqrt{4-x^2}}$

Click to see the solution

Step 1: Identify the substitution

We have $\sqrt{4-x^2} = \sqrt{a^2-x^2}$ with $a = 2$. Use $x = 2\sin(\theta)$:

$$x = 2\sin(\theta), \quad dx = 2\cos(\theta)\,d\theta$$

Then:

$$\sqrt{4-x^2} = \sqrt{4 - 4\sin^2(\theta)} = 2\cos(\theta)$$

Step 2: Substitute

$$\int \frac{dx}{x^2\sqrt{4-x^2}} = \int \frac{2\cos(\theta)\,d\theta}{4\sin^2(\theta) \cdot 2\cos(\theta)}$$ $$= \int \frac{d\theta}{4\sin^2(\theta)}$$ $$= \frac{1}{4}\int \csc^2(\theta)\,d\theta$$

Step 3: Integrate

$$\int \csc^2(\theta)\,d\theta = -\cot(\theta) + C$$

So:

$$= -\frac{1}{4}\cot(\theta) + C$$

Step 4: Back-substitute

From $x = 2\sin(\theta)$, we have $\sin(\theta) = \frac{x}{2}$.

Using the right triangle: opposite $= x$, hypotenuse $= 2$, adjacent $= \sqrt{4-x^2}$:

$$\cot(\theta) = \frac{\sqrt{4-x^2}}{x}$$

Therefore:

$$= -\frac{1}{4} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{4-x^2}}{x} + C$$

Final Answer:

$$\boxed{\int \frac{dx}{x^2\sqrt{4-x^2}} = -\frac{\sqrt{4-x^2}}{4x} + C}$$

Example 1.3

Problem: Evaluate $\int \frac{x^3\,dx}{\sqrt{4-x^2}}$

Click to see the solution

Step 1: Identify the substitution

Use $x = 2\sin(\theta)$, $dx = 2\cos(\theta)\,d\theta$, and $\sqrt{4-x^2} = 2\cos(\theta)$:

Step 2: Substitute

$$\int \frac{x^3\,dx}{\sqrt{4-x^2}} = \int \frac{(2\sin(\theta))^3 \cdot 2\cos(\theta)\,d\theta}{2\cos(\theta)}$$ $$= \int \frac{8\sin^3(\theta) \cdot 2\cos(\theta)\,d\theta}{2\cos(\theta)}$$ $$= \int 8\sin^3(\theta)\,d\theta$$ $$= 8\int \sin^2(\theta)\sin(\theta)\,d\theta$$

Step 3: Use the identity $\sin^2(\theta) = 1 - \cos^2(\theta)$

$$= 8\int (1 - \cos^2(\theta))\sin(\theta)\,d\theta$$

Step 4: Use substitution

Let $u = \cos(\theta)$, then $du = -\sin(\theta)\,d\theta$:

$$= 8\int (1-u^2)(-du)$$ $$= 8\int (u^2 - 1)\,du$$ $$= 8\left[\frac{u^3}{3} - u\right] + C$$ $$= \frac{8u^3}{3} - 8u + C$$

Step 5: Back-substitute

From $x = 2\sin(\theta)$, we have $\sin(\theta) = \frac{x}{2}$.

Using the right triangle: $\cos(\theta) = \frac{\sqrt{4-x^2}}{2}$:

$$= \frac{8}{3}\left(\frac{\sqrt{4-x^2}}{2}\right)^3 - 8 \cdot \frac{\sqrt{4-x^2}}{2} + C$$ $$= \frac{8}{3} \cdot \frac{(4-x^2)^{3/2}}{8} - 4\sqrt{4-x^2} + C$$ $$= \frac{(4-x^2)^{3/2}}{3} - 4\sqrt{4-x^2} + C$$

Final Answer:

$$\boxed{\int \frac{x^3\,dx}{\sqrt{4-x^2}} = \frac{(4-x^2)^{3/2}}{3} - 4\sqrt{4-x^2} + C}$$

Integration by Parts Review

Integration by parts is essential for products of algebraic and transcendental functions. The formula is $\int u\,dv = uv - \int v\,du$.

Example 2.1

Problem: Evaluate $\int x \ln(x)\,dx$

Click to see the solution

Step 1: Choose u and dv

Using LIATE: Logarithmic comes before Algebraic.

$$u = \ln(x), \quad dv = x\,dx$$

Step 2: Compute du and v

$$du = \frac{1}{x}\,dx, \quad v = \frac{x^2}{2}$$

Step 3: Apply integration by parts

$$\int x \ln(x)\,dx = \ln(x) \cdot \frac{x^2}{2} - \int \frac{x^2}{2} \cdot \frac{1}{x}\,dx$$ $$= \frac{x^2}{2}\ln(x) - \frac{1}{2}\int x\,dx$$ $$= \frac{x^2}{2}\ln(x) - \frac{1}{2} \cdot \frac{x^2}{2} + C$$ $$= \frac{x^2}{2}\ln(x) - \frac{x^2}{4} + C$$

Final Answer:

$$\boxed{\int x \ln(x)\,dx = \frac{x^2}{4}\left(2\ln(x) - 1\right) + C}$$

Example 2.2

Problem: Evaluate $\int e^x \sin(x)\,dx$

Click to see the solution

Step 1: First integration by parts

Choose $u = \sin(x)$ and $dv = e^x\,dx$:

$$du = \cos(x)\,dx, \quad v = e^x$$ $$\int e^x \sin(x)\,dx = e^x \sin(x) - \int e^x \cos(x)\,dx$$

Step 2: Second integration by parts

For $\int e^x \cos(x)\,dx$, choose $u = \cos(x)$ and $dv = e^x\,dx$:

$$du = -\sin(x)\,dx, \quad v = e^x$$ $$\int e^x \cos(x)\,dx = e^x \cos(x) - \int e^x (-\sin(x))\,dx$$ $$= e^x \cos(x) + \int e^x \sin(x)\,dx$$

Step 3: Solve for the original integral

Let $I = \int e^x \sin(x)\,dx$. Then:

$$I = e^x \sin(x) - \left(e^x \cos(x) + I\right)$$ $$I = e^x \sin(x) - e^x \cos(x) - I$$ $$2I = e^x(\sin(x) - \cos(x))$$ $$I = \frac{e^x}{2}(\sin(x) - \cos(x))$$

Final Answer:

$$\boxed{\int e^x \sin(x)\,dx = \frac{e^x}{2}(\sin(x) - \cos(x)) + C}$$

Partial Fractions Preview

Partial fractions decomposition allows us to integrate rational functions by expressing them as a sum of simpler fractions.

Partial Fractions Framework

For a proper rational function $\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}$ where $\deg(P) < \deg(Q)$:

  • Factor the denominator $Q(x)$ into linear and irreducible quadratic factors.
  • For each linear factor $(x - a)^n$, write terms: $\frac{A_1}{x-a} + \frac{A_2}{(x-a)^2} + \cdots + \frac{A_n}{(x-a)^n}$.
  • For each irreducible quadratic factor $(x^2 + bx + c)^m$, write terms: $\frac{B_1x+C_1}{x^2+bx+c} + \cdots + \frac{B_mx+C_m}{(x^2+bx+c)^m}$.
  • Clear denominators and solve for the unknown coefficients.

Example 3.1

Problem: Evaluate $\int \frac{dx}{x^2 - 1}$

Figure — Partial fractions regions

The integrand $\frac{1}{x^2-1}$ has vertical asymptotes at $x=\pm 1$ from the partial fractions decomposition.

Click to see the solution

Step 1: Factor the denominator

$$x^2 - 1 = (x-1)(x+1)$$

Step 2: Set up the partial fraction decomposition

$$\frac{1}{x^2-1} = \frac{A}{x-1} + \frac{B}{x+1}$$

Step 3: Clear denominators

$$1 = A(x+1) + B(x-1)$$

Step 4: Solve for A and B

Set $x = 1$: $1 = A(2) + B(0) \implies A = \frac{1}{2}$

Set $x = -1$: $1 = A(0) + B(-2) \implies B = -\frac{1}{2}$

Therefore:

$$\frac{1}{x^2-1} = \frac{1/2}{x-1} - \frac{1/2}{x+1}$$

Step 5: Integrate

$$\int \frac{dx}{x^2-1} = \frac{1}{2}\int \frac{dx}{x-1} - \frac{1}{2}\int \frac{dx}{x+1}$$ $$= \frac{1}{2}\ln|x-1| - \frac{1}{2}\ln|x+1| + C$$

Final Answer:

$$\boxed{\int \frac{dx}{x^2-1} = \frac{1}{2}\ln\left|\frac{x-1}{x+1}\right| + C}$$

Example 3.2

Problem: Evaluate $\int \frac{x+1}{x^2(x-1)}\,dx$

Click to see the solution

Step 1: Set up the partial fraction decomposition

For the repeated linear factor $x^2$ and the factor $(x-1)$:

$$\frac{x+1}{x^2(x-1)} = \frac{A}{x} + \frac{B}{x^2} + \frac{C}{x-1}$$

Step 2: Clear denominators

$$x+1 = A x(x-1) + B(x-1) + C x^2$$

Step 3: Solve for coefficients

Set $x = 0$: $1 = A(0) + B(-1) + C(0) \implies B = -1$

Set $x = 1$: $2 = A(0) + B(0) + C(1) \implies C = 2$

Expand and equate coefficients for $x^2$: $0 = A + C \implies A = -2$

Step 4: Write the decomposition

$$\frac{x+1}{x^2(x-1)} = \frac{-2}{x} - \frac{1}{x^2} + \frac{2}{x-1}$$

Step 5: Integrate term by term

$$\int \frac{x+1}{x^2(x-1)}\,dx = -2\int \frac{dx}{x} - \int x^{-2}\,dx + 2\int \frac{dx}{x-1}$$ $$= -2\ln|x| + \frac{1}{x} + 2\ln|x-1| + C$$

Final Answer:

$$\boxed{\int \frac{x+1}{x^2(x-1)}\,dx = 2\ln\left|\frac{x-1}{x}\right| + \frac{1}{x} + C}$$

Study Tips

1. When choosing a trigonometric substitution, first rewrite the integral to match one of the three standard forms: $\sqrt{a^2-x^2}$, $\sqrt{a^2+x^2}$, or $\sqrt{x^2-a^2}$.

2. Always draw a right triangle after making a trig substitution. It is the fastest way to back-substitute and express the answer in terms of $x$.

3. For integration by parts, if applying the formula once returns the original integral (like with $e^x\sin(x)$), solve algebraically for the integral rather than integrating by parts again.

4. Before attempting partial fractions, confirm the rational function is proper. If the numerator degree is greater than or equal to the denominator, perform polynomial long division first.
